What battery wear really costs a trading bot.

Every time a battery charges and discharges, it wears out a little. I ran my trading agent through a full year of real 2025 prices, with and without a price on that wear. The result wasn't what I expected.

Reading time
5 min
Data

AI Grid Trader

The price of one cycle

A battery's cells lose a little capacity every time they charge and discharge. The demo assumes cells last about 6,000 full cycles before they're down to 80% of their capacity, so each cycle uses up 1/6,000th of that 20%, and replacing it means buying new cells. For a 100 MWh battery in Germany, with cells at about €120 per kWh:

wear per cycle = 20% × 100,000 kWh × €120/kWh ÷ 6,000 cycles = €400

A full cycle moves 100 MWh in and 100 MWh out, so every MWh moved costs €2. Buying power and selling it back has to earn more than €4 per MWh, on top of the 12% lost on the round trip, before it's worth doing.

Leaving wear out flatters everyone

The easiest mistake is to report trading revenue and stop there. Here's a full year in Germany, for my agent and for a simple rule that buys when the price is below its 3-hour average and sells when it's above:

For the agent, wear is about 6% of revenue. For the rule-based bot it's much worse: it already loses money, and wear makes that loss 56% bigger, because it runs 31% more cycles than the agent to earn less. A strategy that trades a lot looks far better on a revenue chart than on a bank statement.

Does the agent need to know?

The more interesting question is whether the agent itself has to know about wear. I ran the same agent twice in each market: once with wear priced into its reward, once with wear set to zero. Then I charged both for the wear they actually caused.

Pricing wear cut cycles by 6 to 13%, and the money barely moved: the agent that ignored wear came out ahead by 0.2 to 0.3%. That's within the noise of the model's other assumptions, such as the small penalty for starting a new charge or discharge run and how energy left in the battery at the end is valued. With cells this cheap, the trades a wear price removes are the marginal ones: they only just cover their own wear.

So why price wear at all?

  • Warranties count cycles. Grid batteries are typically warrantied for about 365 full cycles a year. Pricing wear keeps the agent closer to that budget: in South Australia it saves 64 cycles a year, about two months of the allowance.
  • It's a dial, not a constant. If cells cost more, or the warranty is tighter, the wear price goes up and the agent gets pickier on its own. The demo has a slider for it.
  • Honest reporting. The first table is the real lesson: always quote results after wear. It's the difference between a bot that looks clever and one that makes money.

What I'd check next

The ledger charges wear in proportion to the energy moved. Real cells also age with time, temperature, depth of discharge and how fast they're charged. Modelling those would make wear depend on how the battery is cycled, not just how much, and I expect it would penalise the bot's frequent small trades even more.
