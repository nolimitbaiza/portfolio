Work/Case study

AI Grid Trader: teaching a battery when not to trade.

An AI agent that trades a 100 MWh grid battery on a full year of real wholesale electricity prices in California, Germany and South Australia. It only sees a forecast, it pays for its own battery wear, and it is scored against both a simple trading rule and the theoretical best.

01Problem

Cheap power, expensive cycles.

Why I built this

I'm studying computer science because I want to work on the software side of the energy transition. Batteries kept coming up as the piece that makes solar and wind usable around the clock, but most explanations of how they earn money stopped at "buy low, sell high". I wanted to find out what that really takes: how a battery should decide, what wear really costs, and whether a smart agent beats a simple rule on real prices. Building it end to end, from raw market data to a deployed app, was also the best way I knew to learn the whole stack.

Batteries are what let a grid run on more solar and wind. They soak up power when it's plentiful and cheap, and give it back at the evening peak. The owner earns the price gap.

But every charge and discharge wears out the cells, and nobody knows tomorrow's prices for sure. The two obvious strategies both fail:

  • Trade every price wiggle, and the wear eats the profit and shortens the battery's life.
  • Follow a simple rule (buy below the recent average, sell above it), and you trade far too often on spreads that don't cover the wear.

So the question I set out to answer: on real prices, with only a forecast and a fair charge for wear, how much can a battery actually earn, and how close is that to the best possible?

02Approach

Plan like an operator, pay for wear.

A decision every 15 minutes

I framed dispatch as a Markov decision process. The state is (time, state of charge, current mode), the action is how much to charge, discharge or hold, and the reward is:

reward = arbitrage cash − battery wear − micro-cycling penalty

The decision table is solved exactly with dynamic programming (Bellman updates). That's the same answer a fully trained Q-learning agent converges to, but in milliseconds and identical on every run, which matters when you're showing it to someone.

Wear is a cost, not an afterthought

Every MWh moved is charged its share of a cycle's wear: about €400 per cycle in Germany ($500 in California, A$900 in South Australia), the price of new cells for the capacity a cycle uses up. A trade whose price gap doesn't beat that is simply skipped. A small penalty for starting a new charge or discharge run makes the agent commit to a few clean cycles instead of chasing noise.

No peeking at the future

  1. 1Get a fresh 24-hour price forecast
  2. 2Re-plan the whole day with dynamic programming
  3. 3Execute only the next hour
  4. 4Score it on the prices that actually happened

The simulated forecast behaves like a real one: it misses short spikes, flattens peaks and dips further ahead, and its errors are correlated. A slider in the demo sets how wrong it is.

All three strategies use the same accounting, on a full calendar year of real 2025 prices in 15-minute steps: CAISO SP15 (California), Germany-Luxembourg day-ahead via Energy-Charts, and AEMO SA1 (South Australia).

What I took away

  • A forecast is enough. Seeing only a forecast, the agent still captures 93–95% of what perfect hindsight could earn.
  • Knowing when not to trade is the edge. The rule-based bot loses money over a year in California and Germany, and over the project's life in all three markets, mostly by cycling on spreads that don't pay for the wear.
  • The market matters more than the algorithm. The same battery pays back in about 5 years in South Australia and doesn't cover its cost of capital in California at 2025 prices, even with the tax credit.
  • Wear pricing is visible. Drag the demo's wear cost to zero and the agent starts micro-cycling straight away.

8% discount rate, 2025 benchmark installed costs. Results are illustrative: forecast error is simulated, and past prices don't guarantee future revenue.

05What's next

What I'd build next.

  1. A real price forecaster. Forecast error is simulated today. Training a model on load, wind and solar forecasts would show how much of the remaining 5–7% gap to hindsight a better forecast closes.
  2. Physics-based degradation. Wear is a flat cost per cycle. Making it depend on depth of discharge, state of charge and temperature would change which cycles are worth taking.
  3. Co-optimising grid services. Power for frequency services is reserved up front. Deciding hour by hour whether a megawatt earns more in trading or in services should lift revenue.
  4. Financing and taxes. The lifetime model leaves out debt, taxes and residual value, which a real investment case needs.

Markets and days

Simulated
Four made-up 48-hour price patterns, in dollars. Typical day has morning and evening peaks with a midday solar dip. Volatile adds more noise and price spikes. Solar surplus has a deep midday dip. Heatwave has a very high evening peak. They are the quickest way to see the idea.
Real prices
California (CAISO SP15, $), Germany (day-ahead, €) and South Australia (AEMO SA1, A$): every 15-minute price of 2025. The chart shows two days; the full-year section uses all of them. Each market brings its own installed cost, cost of new cells, tax credit, grid-services price and capacity payment, and a wear cost to match.
Date menu, New day N
For a real market, the date menu picks which 48 hours to show, and New day moves to the next two days. For a simulated market, New day makes a fresh price pattern of the same kind.
Upload CSV
Runs the agent on any market's prices. Use a CSV with a price column, ideally with a timestamp column, in 5, 15, 30 or 60-minute steps and at least 48 hours long; commas or semicolons, decimal points or commas all work. Four weeks or more also gets the full-year view, scaled to 365 days. The file is read in your browser and never uploaded.

Why this trade?

Click any ▲ or ▼, or explain the first trade under the chart, and a panel explains that decision:

  • What it did: how many MW it bought or sold, at what price and when.
  • Why: for a buy, the price its forecast expected when it planned to sell that energy; for a sell, what the energy cost and whether higher prices were coming.
  • The numbers: cost per stored MWh and the sale price, both after efficiency losses, the battery wear per MWh, and the margin left over.
  • What really happened, since the forecast can be wrong, and what the rule-based bot did at the same moment.

Previous trade and Next trade step through every decision in order, and × closes the panel.

Forecast or perfect hindsight H

The Agent sees switch above the chart sets what the AI knows. Forecast is the realistic case: it plans on an imperfect forecast, re-plans every hour and is scored on the prices that actually happened. Perfect hindsight lets it see every future price, which is the most any strategy could earn. Switching between the two shows what forecast error costs.

Settings

The first four sliders sit under the chart, and the rest open under More settings. The AI re-plans as soon as you move one.

Battery size
How much energy the battery holds, in MWh. Default 100 MWh.
Power rating
How fast it can charge or discharge, in MW. Size divided by power is how long a full charge lasts: 100 MWh at 25 MW is a 4-hour battery.
Wear cost per cycle
What one full cycle costs in lost battery life. The AI only trades when the price gap beats it; at zero it trades on every small wiggle.
Forecast error
How wrong the forecast is, from none to ±40%. Like a real forecast, its errors grow further ahead, miss short spikes and flatten peaks and dips.
Price volatility
Simulated markets only: how much random noise is added to the prices.
Round-trip efficiency
The share of energy that comes back out after charging; the rest is lost as heat. Lower efficiency needs bigger price gaps.
Micro-cycling penalty
A charge for starting each new charge or discharge run, so the AI commits to a few clean cycles instead of many short ones.
Installed cost
What the battery cost to build, per kWh. It sets ROI, payback and lifetime value, not the trading decisions.
Tax credit on installed cost
A share of the installed cost paid back up front, like the US investment tax credit (30% by default in California).
Lowest and highest charge level
The range the battery is kept within, which protects the cells. Default 10–90%.

How it compares

A table puts the AI, perfect hindsight and the rule-based bot side by side on the same 48 hours: trading revenue, battery wear, net profit, cycles used and charge and discharge runs. The Profit after battery wear chart next to it shows each one's running total.

A full year on real prices

For the real markets, and an uploaded CSV of four weeks or more, the same agent then trades every hour of the year in the background, re-planning on a 24-hour forecast each time. A progress line shows how far it has got, and the page stays usable. When it finishes you get:

  • Net revenue for the year, the gain over the bot, the share of the hindsight maximum, and annual ROI with payback time.
  • Net revenue per month as bars against the hindsight maximum as a line, which shows the seasons.
  • A table of revenue, wear, cycles and runs per year for all three strategies.

Revenue streams

A battery can earn more than trading. This section adds up a year's income in a bar and a table, with the annual ROI for all streams and for trading alone. Four sliders set it up:

Power reserved for grid services
The share of power kept back for frequency services. It is paid for being available, and can't be used for trading.
Grid services price
What frequency services pay, per MW per hour. Each real market starts at its 2025 average.
Capacity payment
A yearly payment per kW for being available at peak times. Batteries shorter than 4 hours get part of it. Germany and South Australia have none.
Co-located solar
Solar panels at the same site. The battery stores the midday output the inverter can't export, for free. The solar output is simulated, typical for the region.

Battery lifetime

This turns one year into the whole project: cash flow for every year of the battery's life, with the cells wearing out and new ones bought when health drops below 80%. It shows the lifetime value (NPV), internal rate of return, payback time and how many times new cells are needed, each next to the bot's, plus charts of battery health and cumulative cash flow.

Project life
How many years the project runs, from 10 to 20.
Discount rate
How much less future cash is worth today; it sets the NPV.
Cycle life (to 80% health)
How many full cycles the cells last before they hold 20% less energy.
Cost of new cells
The price of replacement cells per kWh, which falls 3% a year.

The summary underneath compares the cycles a year with a typical warranty limit of 365, and works out what wear really costs per cycle under these assumptions. If that differs from the agent's wear price, a Use … button sets the agent to the physical figure and it re-plans.

Sharing and presenting

Share
Copies a link that stores every setting, so whoever opens it sees exactly your scenario. On a phone it opens the share menu. An uploaded CSV isn't included.
Business case
A one-page PDF of the scenario on screen. Enter who it's for, the project, your name and contact, then choose Save as PDF in the print window. Nothing is uploaded.
Present F
A full-screen walkthrough in large type, one step at a time: the prices, the AI's first buy and first sell with the reason for each, the rest of the 48 hours, the result against the bot, the full year, the lifetime, and a closing screen with QR codes. A presentation clicker works too.
Auto-play K
Plays every market in turn while the numbers count up, for a screen at a stand. For a screen that runs all day, open ai-grid-trader?kiosk=1: it starts by itself, hands control to anyone who taps, and starts again after a minute with no one using it.
Light or dark T
The sun and moon button switches theme, and your choice is remembered.
Works offline
Open the demo once while online and it opens later with no connection. You can also install it like an app: the install icon in Chrome or Edge's address bar, or Share → Add to Home Screen on iPhone and iPad.
